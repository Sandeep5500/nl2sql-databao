# Teach the judge where majorities go wrong

The research supports building most of the judge's training set from **our own agent's sampled answers on non-Spider2 questions, filtered toward pools where a lone correct answer faces an agreeing wrong majority, with every "correct" label in those pools checked a second time**, and topping it up with a smaller set of Spider2-style questions written on other SQLite databases whose wrong candidates are mutations that execution proves differ from a verified gold. On-policy data matters because judges transfer poorly across generators: a verifier trained on Codex samples scored 46.4 on InCoder's samples against 54.1 when trained on InCoder's own ([LEVER](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2302.08468)), and in Meta's Principia study, judges trained on other models' answers ended *below the untrained judge* once used to train a policy while the on-policy judge led (40.41–41.08 vs 41.88 vs 43.41) ([Principia](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.18886)). Labels are the other constraint, since at least 52.1% of audited BIRD-train gold SQL is wrong ([ReViSQL](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.20004v2)) and SynSQL-2.5M pairs two-or-three-row tables with 8-sample majority-vote labels that reward the popular wrong answers our judge must learn to reject ([OmniSQL](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.02240)). Public data becomes usable once ten databases holding the same data as 43 of the 135 local questions (49 counting partial matches) and a deny-list of Spider2-derived sets are removed, and LiveSQLBench's SQLite release plus BIRD Mini-Interact are the best non-Spider2 test sets. Training should use GRPO with a reward for verdicts that hold in both presentation orders, on roughly 10–25K pairs, with thinking on and the strongest base model you can afford. **Expect +1 to +4 questions over 82–83**: published trained selectors close roughly 15–35% of the gap between majority voting and the oracle, our zero-shot judge already closes about 41%, and a training-free 35B+JEV ensemble has already reached 85. A gain that size sits at or below the ~3-point noise floor of the 135, so the go/no-go decision has to rest on a non-Spider2 evaluation whose databases never appear in training.

## Synthetic pipelines supply volume, but judge labels need proof

Text-to-SQL synthesis has settled on one shape: write SQL against a database, execute it to drop failures, then write a question from the SQL. OmniSQL's SynSQL-2.5M is the largest example, with **2.54M samples over 16,583 LLM-built SQLite databases**, generated entirely by open-weight models and released under Apache-2.0 ([SynSQL card](https://huggingface.co/datasets/seeklhy/SynSQL-2.5M)). Three of its design choices make it a weak source of judge pairs. Its tables hold only the rows the schema-writing model invented, and a team inspection of 30 databases found **no table with more than three rows and six databases with none** ([SynSQL card](https://huggingface.co/datasets/seeklhy/SynSQL-2.5M)); because wrong grain, join fan-out, name keys and ties need several rows per entity before they change a result, most wrong queries on these databases return the gold's rows or nothing. Its SQL averages 57 whitespace tokens against 144.5 for Spider 2.0-Lite, with window functions in about 1.3% of queries ([OmniSQL](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.02240); [Spider 2.0](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.07763)). And its final SQL is re-derived by sampling eight solutions and keeping the largest execution group, a majority vote that leans toward exactly the answers our judge must learn to reject. A human audit rated **86% of samples fully correct** ([OmniSQL](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.02240)), so unchecked SynSQL gold would mislabel roughly one pair in seven. The other pipelines trade volume, realism and label quality differently.

| Pipeline | Where the gold comes from | Measured label quality | Value for judge pairs |
|---|---|---|---|
| SynSQL-2.5M ([OmniSQL](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.02240)) | LLM writes SQL by complexity tier on LLM-made databases; 8-sample execution majority | 86% of 1,000 audited samples fully correct | SQL text only, until the databases are re-populated |
| SQL-Factory ([paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.14837v5)) | GPT-4o explores, Qwen2.5-Coder-14B expands and critiques, on real schemas | 4.6% of 1,000 audited pairs inconsistent; 15.34% return empty results | Real rows, but built on Spider and BIRD schemas that overlap ours; about $180 for 380K queries |
| RingSQL ([paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.05451)) | 32 templates compiled deterministically into SQL | SQL correct by construction; question fidelity about 75 (UniEval) | Clean positives; complexity capped by the templates |
| ScienceBenchmark ([paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.04743)) | Expert templates filled from the database; GPT-3 writes the questions | 75–83% question–SQL equivalence | Shows that question wording is the noisy side |
| SWE-SQL SQL-Rewind ([paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.18951)) | LLM injects plausible bugs into verified SQL; kept only if tests pass the fix and fail the bug | No human verification | Closest existing near-miss generator |
| Gretel ([card](https://huggingface.co/datasets/gretelai/synthetic_text_to_sql)) | Undisclosed LLMs; one-table contexts | Parser checks and a GPT-4 judge only | 22.3% of sampled SELECTs fail in SQLite; poor fit |

Label noise rises with complexity, and nothing in this literature certifies gold at Spider 2.0 difficulty. The cleanest audited set, SQL-Factory, runs modest SQL on familiar schemas; the CTE-heavy SynSQL sits at 14% errors; Spider 2.0's own eight expert annotators found errors in **45% of examples at first validation** ([Spider 2.0](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.07763)); and a later audit judged **62.8% of the checkable Spider 2.0-Snow golds wrong** ([Jin et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.08778)). LLM reviewers flag but cannot certify, as an o3 reviewer reached 90.6% precision but only **24.5% recall** on BIRD-train errors ([ReViSQL](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.20004v2)). The verification methods with the strongest records are correctness by construction, as in RingSQL; execution made discriminative, as in test-suite accuracy, which builds "neighbor" queries by dropping a WHERE clause or swapping > for ≥ and then distils random databases until they separate over 99% of those neighbors from the gold ([Zhong, Yu & Klein](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.02840)); and independent re-derivation such as GAZP's cycle consistency ([GAZP](https://aclanthology.org/2020.emnlp-main.558)). Majority vote ranks last for this project because it reproduces shared errors.

Synthetic training has moved the 135-question Spider 2.0-SQLite set by a handful of questions at most. OmniSQL-7B trained on SynSQL alone scored 9.6% against 3.0% for Spider+BIRD, about 13 questions versus 4 ([OmniSQL](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.02240)), and under reinforcement learning RingSQL's appendix shows SynSQL's complex tier at 11.11% against 8.15% with no added data, 15 questions versus 11, numbers its authors call too low to compare ([RingSQL](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.05451)). Snowflake found SynSQL with model filtering "inconclusive" and unfiltered Gretel slightly harmful (BIRD dev 64.9 to 64.6), while keeping only Gretel items that at least 1 of 10 samples solved lifted it to 66.5 ([Arctic-Text2SQL-R1](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.20315)). The largest data effect on Spider 2.0 in these papers came from verification rather than volume: 2.5K expert-corrected BIRD items added **+13.9 greedy points on Spider 2-Snow** over the original labels ([ReViSQL](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.20004v2)). No paper trains a SQL judge or selector on synthetic pairs and evaluates selection on Spider 2.0-Lite, so any claim about that combination is extrapolation.

The judge-training literature outside SQL supplies the recipe that does carry over. Almost every judge in a verifiable domain is trained by sampling many answers per problem from one model, labelling each by checking it, keeping problems with mixed correctness, pairing correct with incorrect answers, and keeping a teacher's rationale only when its verdict agrees with the label; J1 trained on 22K such pairs ([J1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.10320)), PairJudge kept 1.3M of 2.2M teacher comparisons ([PairJudge](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.13007)), and GenSelect dropped every selection that picked a wrong solution ([AIMO-2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.16891)). Constructed negatives fill specific holes. Self-Taught Evaluator answers a slightly altered version of each instruction and uses that answer as the loser, which lifted Llama3-70B from 75.4 to 88.3 on RewardBench without human labels ([Self-Taught Evaluator](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.02666)); rewriting a question at a different grain, denominator or answer shape is the SQL equivalent. CriticGPT's inserted bugs were harder to catch but "not from the natural distribution of model errors" ([CriticGPT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.00215)), and adding truncated answers as negatives cut judge false positives from as high as 80% to near zero ([Master-RM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.08794)). Constructed negatives therefore belong as a targeted minority of the data, not its bulk.

Three gaps line up with our error list. No pipeline generates negatives for ties at a cutoff or for single-row versus ranked-list answers; the largest SQL error taxonomy, NL2SQL-Bugs, has no category for either, and every model it tested identified join-type and window-function errors 0% of the time ([NL2SQL-Bugs](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.11984)). No pipeline writes Spider2-style definition documents whose clauses the SQL provably depends on, since training pipelines emit one-line evidence strings and only LiveSQLBench's benchmark-side knowledge graph comes close ([BIRD-Interact](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.05318)). And because Spider 2.0-Lite questions were deliberately written for unambiguity rather than naturalness ([Spider 2.0](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.07763)), SynSQL's "vague" and "metaphorical" styles train the wrong kind of difficulty. The strongest pair the literature supports is a certified gold against a known perturbation that returns a different result on a populated database, a pair that stays correctly ordered even if the question wording is slightly off.

## Public data works once ten shared databases are removed

### Ten databases and every Spider2 derivative must go

The largest contamination risk is shared databases, not shared question text. Ten of the 30 local databases hold the same data as databases in BIRD or Spider 1.0, and **43 of the 135 local questions sit on them**; Six-Gym's copy of the Airlines demo adds 2 more, and a partial match between BIRD's legislator database and city_legislation adds 4, for 49 (counts from [spider2-lite.jsonl](https://github.com/xlang-ai/Spider2/blob/main/spider2-lite/spider2-lite.jsonl); matches checked against [BIRD train](https://huggingface.co/datasets/xu3kev/BIRD-SQL-data-train), [Spider](https://huggingface.co/datasets/xlangai/spider) and [Six-Gym](https://huggingface.co/datasets/birdsql/six-gym-sqlite)). ### BIRD and BEAVER can carry the training load

**BIRD train fits the judge's input best**, with real rows to preview, an evidence sentence that plays the role of a definition document, SQLite, and about 64 databases after removals. Its SQL is short, though, at about 25 tokens with no CTEs in train, and its gold is noisy: a 2.5K-item audit found at least **52.1% wrong gold SQL**, a floor given the flagger's recall ([ReViSQL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.20004)), and 293 of retail_world's 373 gold queries reference columns its shipped schema lacks ([BIRD train](https://bird-bench.oss-cn-beijing.aliyuncs.com/train.zip)). **BIRD-Platinum (2,088 train / 374 validation)** gives clean labels, and **bird23-train-filtered minus the overlapping databases (5,767, or 5,639 without legislator)** gives volume, though that filter only removes rows and corrects nothing ([bird23-train-filtered](https://huggingface.co/datasets/birdsql/bird23-train-filtered)). **BEAVER v3 public** is the only public source with gold at Spider2 scale, with **7,978 queries averaging 316.7 tokens and 3.7 CTEs** over schemas of 100+ tables, an MIT tag, no overlap, and a May 2026 release that postdates the Qwen3.5 and Qwen3.6 models in use ([BEAVER v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.02038v3); [HF](https://huggingface.co/api/datasets/beaverbench/beaver-query)). The earlier internal verdict that BEAVER is unusable is therefore out of date; its real costs are a MySQL dialect, 97% LLM recomposition of log-mined templates, and only three public database groups.

Smaller supplements each come with a caveat. Six-Gym SQLite contributes 3,821 buggy-versus-fixed pairs after removals, but it is LLM-made and unverified. NL2SQL-Bugs contributes 1,578 labelled candidates, but because it sits on BIRD dev databases it cannot be training data if BIRD dev is a test set. EHRSQL is a natural source of ties, since its authors fixed 1,840 tie-breaking queries ([changelog](https://github.com/glee4810/EHRSQL/blob/main/dataset/ehrsql/CHANGELOG.md)), and AMBROSIA is the only release whose multiple executable golds return different results, used locally in honour of its authors' request not to re-upload it. Gretel, SEDE, BookSQL and the Spider-family training splits add little, offering respectively single-table contexts with SQLite failures, no database to execute against, non-commercial terms, and easy, highly exposed questions.

### LiveSQLBench and Mini-Interact make the cleanest test

**LiveSQLBench Base-Lite-SQLite (270 tasks) and BIRD Mini-Interact (300 SQLite tasks)** are the closest non-Spider2 analogues, with about 360-token SQL, knowledge-base documents, business domains, no overlap, and gold sent by email to keep it out of crawls ([LiveSQLBench](https://github.com/bird-bench/livesqlbench); [Mini-Interact](https://huggingface.co/datasets/birdsql/mini-interact)); whether Mini-Interact reuses Base-Lite's databases should be checked before treating them as independent. Three conflicts follow from the choice of test sets. Reserving BIRD dev 1106, which changed 16.5% of gold results from its predecessor, rules out training on NL2SQL-Bugs and BIRD-Critic, which share its databases. ARCS draws on the BIRD-train databases retails and professional_basketball ([ARCS](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.09396)), so those two leave training if ARCS is a test. And ARCS's conventions, which return all tied rows and never multiply percentages by 100, test judge behavior rather than Spider2's conventions.

No published study measures memorization of text-to-SQL benchmarks by any Qwen model. Qwen3.5 and Qwen3.6 have no technical report or data cutoff, Qwen2.5-Coder decontaminated only against HumanEval, MBPP, GSM8K and MATH ([Qwen2.5-Coder](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.12186)), and Qwen2.5-Math-7B reproduced the rest of a MATH-500 problem exactly 54.6% of the time from a 60% prefix ([Wu et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.10532)). The evaluation set itself is exposed, since the Genies rollouts appeared two months before the Qwen3.6-35B-A3B repository was created ([model card](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3.6-35B-A3B)). Exposure matters less for training data than for evaluation, so the practical response is to run a masked-column reconstruction probe ([Ranaldi et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.08100)) and a partial-question completion probe on the agent and the judge's base, with LiveSQLBench Large-v1 or ARCS as post-cutoff controls, and to report the 43–49 questions on overlapping databases separately.

## Spider2-style items should be built on data traps and proven by execution

### The target is long, explicit analysis on small, messy databases

The local split is hard because of query logic and data quirks, not schema size: its 30 databases have a **median of 13 tables and 77 columns**, far below the paper's stated criterion of more than 200 columns ([local schemas](/Users/deepikapalagani/Documents/nl2sql-databao/Spider2/spider2-lite/resource/databases/sqlite)). Questions average 51 words and read as procedures ([spider2-lite.jsonl](/Users/deepikapalagani/Documents/nl2sql-databao/Spider2/spider2-lite/spider2-lite.jsonl)), and since December 2024, 82 of the 135 have been rewritten, with the changed ones growing from 33.9 to 63.6 words as authors fixed tie-breaks, named output columns and defined status codes ([question history](https://github.com/xlang-ai/Spider2/commits/main/spider2-lite/spider2-lite.jsonl)). Gold token counts average 184 (median 143), and 58 of the 135 sit in the paper's "hard" band of 160 tokens or more ([spider2lite_eval.jsonl](/Users/deepikapalagani/Documents/nl2sql-databao/Spider2/spider2-lite/evaluation_suite/gold/spider2lite_eval.jsonl)); 21 of the 24 public local golds use CTEs, 10 use CASE and 8 use window functions, with heavy use of SUBSTR, INSTR, TRIM and JULIANDAY ([public golds](/Users/deepikapalagani/Documents/nl2sql-databao/Spider2/spider2-lite/evaluation_suite/gold/sql)). Answers are small, a median of 3 rows with **45 of 135 a single row** ([gold results](/Users/deepikapalagani/Documents/nl2sql-databao/Spider2/spider2-lite/evaluation_suite/gold/exec_result)), and 13 tasks carry a short Markdown document of 60–918 words, such as RFM rules, the haversine formula or a label taxonomy ([documents](/Users/deepikapalagani/Documents/nl2sql-databao/Spider2/spider2-lite/resource/documents)). Scoring requires every gold column to match some predicted column, so extra columns pass and extra rows fail; numbers match within an absolute 0.01, NULL counts as 0, `ignore_order` is on for every task, 49 tasks restrict scoring to essential columns, and 91 of the 135 accept several gold variants, up from 21 before a November 2025 re-annotation ([evaluate.py](https://github.com/xlang-ai/Spider2/blob/main/spider2-lite/evaluation_suite/evaluate.py); [commit 5fa8a517](https://github.com/xlang-ai/Spider2/commit/5fa8a5179c411c22ff8fd902c9e01c5bc2bf6c47)). Synthetic items should therefore copy the post-revision style, explicit, column-naming and definition-bearing rather than vague, and should be labelled with this comparator and its variants so the judge learns the conventions it is scored under.

### A trap-first pipeline replaces voting with execution proofs

Benchmark construction shows which steps can be automated. Spider 2.0, BEAVER and BIRD-Interact all used humans for the semantic core, with LLMs only paraphrasing, instantiating log-mined templates under expert review, or simulating users ([BEAVER v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.02038v3); [BIRD-Interact](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.05318v3)), and an o3-based audit agent flagged gold errors at 89% precision with humans adjudicating every flag ([Jin et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.08778v3)). The pipeline proposed here, a synthesis of those sources rather than an evaluated method, therefore automates profiling, drafting, re-solving and mutation, and reserves human or strongest-model time for items where independent solvers disagree. It starts from mid-size relational SQLite databases with messy business data, excluding any whose table and column names overlap a local database above a Jaccard of about 0.5 or whose value fingerprints match one, and holding out 20–30% of databases for validation so that the split is by database. Scripts then profile each database for traps, reusing the repo's key and profile tooling to find fan-out factors per join path, display names shared by several IDs, lexically overlapping status codes, ties at plausible top-k cutoffs, NULLs and zeros in candidate denominators, calendar gaps and text-encoded dates. One model writes gold SQL for an analytic pattern placed on a trap, such as share of total, rate per population, top-k per group, running balance, cohort retention or period-over-period change, as a 100–350-token CTE chain over two to four tables. A different model writes a 40–70-word question from the SQL in the post-revision style, and two independent solvers, ideally from different model families, answer it blind; the item is accepted when a blind solution matches the gold under the official comparator and otherwise goes to an audit agent and adjudication, with a high first-pass error rate expected. Each surviving item must then prove that it discriminates: every error-type mutant must return a different result from the gold and all its variants, and on three to five perturbed copies of the database, each adding one stressor such as an extra child row or a tied value at the cutoff, the gold must change only as its meaning requires while the mutant diverges.

The card below maps each error type to the wrong SQL a majority tends to write and to the data condition without which the mutant is indistinguishable from the gold, drawing on the taxonomies above ([NL2SQL-Bugs](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.11984); [BIRD-Interact](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.05318v3); [ARCS](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.09396)). The errors are realistic: published Spider 2.0-Lite golds themselves contain fan-out ([#216](https://github.com/xlang-ai/Spider2/issues/216)), grain ambiguity ([#159](https://github.com/xlang-ai/Spider2/issues/159)) and tie problems ([#168](https://github.com/xlang-ai/Spider2/issues/168)).

| Error type | Typical majority-wrong SQL | Data condition that makes it change the answer | Closest prior coverage |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wrong grain | One GROUP BY at the wrong level; AVG over raw rows instead of per-entity means | Unequal group sizes; calendar gaps for "each month" questions | NL2SQL-Bugs aggregate and clause errors |
| Wrong denominator | Filter in WHERE shrinks the base; COUNT(col) skips NULLs; SQLite integer division; post-join row counts | Filtered and unfiltered populations differ; NULLs or zeros; integer columns | BIRD-Interact Divide-by-zero, Null |
| Join fan-out | SUM or COUNT after a 1:N join (fan trap) or across two 1:N children (chasm trap) | Some in-scope parents have several children | BIRD-Interact Join, Distinct; audit "row inflation" |
| Overlapping statuses | A single literal; `LIKE '%complete%'` catching "incomplete"; counting every status row instead of the latest | Entities with several status rows; overlapping codes | NL2SQL-Bugs condition and value mismatch |
| Name vs ID | GROUP BY name merges namesakes; joins on names | At least one name shared by two IDs in scope | NL2SQL-Bugs attribute mismatch; ARCS convention |
| Single row vs ranked list | Full ranking where one row is asked; one row where one per group is asked | None; vary the phrasing on the same data | None; Spider 2.0's "answer format" rewrites |
| Ties at a cutoff | `LIMIT k` with arbitrary tie order; ROW_NUMBER where RANK is meant | A tie exactly at position k | BIRD-Interact Rank, Sort; ARCS convention |
| Output formatting | Fraction instead of percentage; missing scale factor; wrong label spelling or date format | None, but must fail at 0.01 tolerance (rounding to 2 decimals passes) | BIRD-Interact Decimal |

Five SQLite behaviors deserve their own mutants because the local split is SQLite: integer division, date functions that return NULL on non-ISO text dates, bare non-aggregated columns in GROUP BY, NULL sort position, and untrimmed or mixed-case string keys. For the roughly 10% of items that should carry a definition document, LiveSQLBench's knowledge-base format, in which each entry has a definition, a type and a list of the entries it depends on, gives a ready schema ([LiveSQLBench card](https://huggingface.co/datasets/birdsql/livesqlbench-base-lite)): generate definition entries first, SQL that combines them second, and negatives that ignore or misapply one entry third, which produces wrong-denominator and overlapping-status errors directly. Candidate sets come last. Our agent runs four times per item, and where its natural errors miss the target failure, mutants fill the gap, ideally two different mutants that return the same wrong result, such as fan-out through two join paths, to imitate an agreeing wrong majority; each pair is rendered exactly as the deployed judge sees it and tagged with its error type.

### Synthesis on the 30 test databases stays a separate, disclosed condition

Generating new questions on the 30 local databases would satisfy the letter of "no Spider 2.0 questions or gold", and no rule found forbids it, but it would teach the judge the test databases' codes, value formats and fan-out paths. The maintainers call Spider 2.0 "only a test set" and rely on academic integrity ([issue #8](https://github.com/xlang-ai/Spider2/issues/8)), and published precedent accepts synthesis on evaluation databases only when disclosed and reported separately: YORO trained per-database experts on synthetic data for its evaluation databases, said so, and reported test-to-synthetic SQL overlap below 0.5% ([YORO](https://aclanthology.org/2025.naacl-long.94.pdf)). For a judge the specific risk is learning which result shapes are typical for each test database and choosing by that prior, so if tried, this belongs in a labelled transductive condition, with some local databases held out of synthesis and gains compared between covered and uncovered databases. Every condition should pass the same filter stack: database name Jaccard and value fingerprints; 13-gram and 8-gram overlap against the 135 questions; a top-5 embedding search with an LLM judging paraphrases, which n-gram filters miss ([LLM decontaminator](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04850)); normalized SQL and skeleton matching against the 24 public golds; and, for test-database synthesis only, hashes of each synthetic result checked against the 135 gold CSVs. One caveat applies throughout: the eight error types were found by reading the judge's failures on the 135, so trap-targeted training is itself test-informed design, one more reason the primary evidence must come from non-Spider2 data.

## On-policy pools of outvoted correct answers should carry the most weight

### Correlated errors make our own agent the right data source

The findings support a combination weighted toward mining our agent's own pools, with verified trap items as a targeted minority that doubles as the evaluation instrument and public near-miss sets as supplements only; raw synthetic corpora, off-policy candidate releases such as GradeSQL's OmniSQL-7B samples, and synthesis on the test databases stay out of the main condition. Beyond LEVER and Principia, the case for mining rests on four results. Negatives from the same strong model beat weaker-model negatives by 1.3 points of pairwise accuracy and **5.2 points of order consistency** ([SFR-Judge](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.14664)); a 72B reward model trained on a different distribution fell below majority voting, 66.8 versus 68.4 ([GenSelect](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.17797)); strong judges score barely above chance on pairs drawn from one strong model ([JudgeBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.12784)); and errors from weak generators are the easiest to catch ([Variation in Verification](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.17995)), while ours is not a weak generator. Our misses look like correlated errors rather than vote-following, because the judge sees distinct result groups rather than vote counts ([judge_pools.py](/Users/deepikapalagani/Documents/nl2sql-databao/nl2sql-v2/scripts/judge_pools.py)) and models increasingly share mistakes ([Great Models Think Alike](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.04313)), so a same-family judge tends to share the agent's misreading of grain or denominator. The harness then turns any hesitation into a loss for the minority: a verdict that flips with presentation order averages to 0.5, and score ties go to the group that more lanes produced ([analyze_pools.py](/Users/deepikapalagani/Documents/nl2sql-databao/nl2sql-v2/scripts/analyze_pools.py)). **Order-consistent accuracy on minority-correct pairs is therefore the exact quantity to train and track.**

### Mining and labelling the pools

No published SQL selector trains deliberately on that slice: CHASE-SQL, Agentar-Scale-SQL, SIRIUS-SQL and R³-SQL build pairs from cluster representatives without vote shares, and none reports how often it overturns a wrong majority ([CHASE-SQL](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.01943); [Agentar](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.24403); [SIRIUS-SQL](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.01246); [R³-SQL](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.25325)). The nearest guidance comes from three results. In math aggregation, AggLM calls a group hard when its majority answer is wrong, keeps all hard groups, adds 5–50% easy ones, and finds hard-only training makes rewards too sparse ([AggLM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.06870)). The training-free SQL method DPC rescued 21.4% of majority-wrong questions while majority-correct ones fell from 100% to 97.0%, which is the false-override cost to budget against ([DPC](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.15163)). And SIRIUS kept 20.3% both-wrong pairs, while R³ found that wrong-versus-wrong matches can lift a wrong group above the correct one. Training therefore needs majority-correct pairs at their natural rate, so the judge does not learn to favor the odd answer out, as well as both-wrong pairs.

Mining means running the deployed agent exactly as deployed, with the same weights, temperature and tool loop and the same DCE enrichment, keys and profile built for every training database ([CLAUDE.md](/Users/deepikapalagani/Documents/nl2sql-databao/CLAUDE.md)), four to eight times per question, then grouping results with the scorer's normalization and keeping pools that mix correct and wrong answers. Ranking each pool's wrong groups by how strongly the current 35B judge prefers them targets its actual errors, as R³ did with its fifteen highest-scoring wrong candidates. Where the agent never finds the answer, CHASE-SQL's trick of hinting the gold SQL to produce a correct candidate raises the yield of minority-correct pools, but the hinted candidate is off-policy, so all SQL should pass through a formatter, as in XiYan-SQL, to strip style cues ([XiYan-SQL](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.04701)).

Labels need more care than candidates. They should come from the Spider2 comparator rather than BIRD-style set equality, which penalizes extra columns, and gold that depends on tie order should be dropped or relabelled, since about a fifth of BIRD dev queries can return different rows depending on how ties break ([Pourreza & Rafiei](https://aclanthology.org/2023.emnlp-main.99)). Every minority-correct pool needs a second check, because disagreement between the agent's majority and the gold is exactly where wrong gold concentrates: CHASE-SQL attributed 10.0% of all BIRD-dev questions, more than a third of its failures, to wrong gold ([CHASE-SQL](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.01943)). Discarding labels that fail the check is affordable, since consensus filtering that kept only about 40% of process labels still improved results ([Qwen PRM lessons](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.07301)). Perturbed database copies catch the opposite error, a fan-out join or name key that matches gold only because this database instance hides the problem, and over-filtered negatives that return too few rows keep "fewer rows wins" from becoming a shortcut. The proposed composition looks like this.

| Layer | Questions | Candidates | Label check | Share (proposal) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mined on-policy pools | BIRD-Platinum; bird23-train-filtered minus overlaps; BEAVER dw; trap items | Our agent, 4–8 samples, same tool loop and context | Spider2 comparator; second check on every minority-correct pool | Most pairs; all majority-wrong pools plus 5–50% easy ones |
| Constructed near-misses | Trap items on non-Spider2 databases | Gold mutants proven to differ, including two agreeing mutants | Discrimination test plus perturbed databases | Minority; ties, answer shape, formatting, documents |
| Both-wrong pairs | Same pools | Distinct wrong groups | Comparator | Present; SIRIUS used 20.3% |
| Held out, never trained | LiveSQLBench SQLite, Mini-Interact, held-out trap databases | Agent samples | Official or emailed gold | Model selection only |

### GRPO with a both-order reward on the strongest base

The literature supports a budget of 10–25K pairs. J1 used 22K; EvalPlanner gained only 0.2 points going from 5K to 22K pairs in one round but 1.4 from a second round ([EvalPlanner](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.18099)); and CHASE-SQL's 3.8K pairs left a tuned Gemma-2-9B level with an untuned Gemini-1.5-Pro, 64.28 versus 63.98 ([CHASE-SQL](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.01943)). The second round should come from the trained judge's own errors on fresh pools. An optional supervised warm start can use the 35B thinking judge's traces, kept only when the verdict matches the label in both orders and never generated with the gold in the prompt, because hinted traces take shortcuts ([DeepSeek-GRM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.02495)). GRPO follows, rewarding a correct verdict with a bonus only when it holds in both orders and keeping a KL penalty. On the same data, J1's GRPO beat DPO (59.2 vs 52.8 at 8B), and its both-order reward raised order-consistent accuracy from 39.1 to 43.9 while cutting verdict flips from 36.8% to 28.7% ([J1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.10320)); in SQL, R³-SQL's swap-consistency GRPO reached **78.86 pair accuracy against 67.66 for supervised fine-tuning** ([R³-SQL](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.25325)).

Thinking should stay on, since turning it off made our 35B judge much worse ([judge findings](/Users/deepikapalagani/.claude/projects/-Users-deepikapalagani-Documents-nl2sql-databao/memory/nl2sql-judge-selection-findings.md)) and small Qwen3 judges gain about 10 points from it ([Explicit Reasoning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.13332)). The base should be the strongest one you can train, the 35B-A3B judge itself or the Qwen3.8-27B base behind JEV-27B, because a 9B judge sits on the wrong side of the generator–verifier gap: verification ability tracks the verifier's own solving ability ([Variation in Verification](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.17995)), and J1 training gave nothing to a 1.7B model judging its own outputs but +10% to a 32B one ([Principia](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.18886)). Training prompts should render exactly what the deployed judge sees, and result previews versus plain SQL should be tested on held-out data, because the evidence is mixed: execution results helped LEVER by 3.0 points, GradeSQL's verifier worked best on SQL alone ([GradeSQL](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.01308v2)), and database context helped our judge on easy pairs while hurting it on hard ones ([judge findings](/Users/deepikapalagani/.claude/projects/-Users-deepikapalagani-Documents-nl2sql-databao/memory/nl2sql-judge-selection-findings.md)). Pairs should be regenerated whenever the agent or its scaffold changes.

## Expect one to four extra questions, proven off Spider 2.0

Published fine-tuned SQL selectors add +1.82 (Agentar-Scale-SQL) to +4.17 (CHASE-SQL) execution-accuracy points over majority voting or self-consistency on BIRD dev, and by my arithmetic close roughly 15–35% of the voting-to-oracle gap: 29.9% for CHASE-SQL, 26.1% for XiYan-SQL, 16.2% for Agentar-Scale-SQL and 34% for MSc-SQL ([CHASE-SQL](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.01943); [XiYan-SQL](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.04701); [Agentar](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.24403); [MSc-SQL](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.12916)). Our zero-shot thinking judge already closes about **41%** on our pools, 296 of 540 correct against 261 for majority voting and 346 for pass@4 ([judge findings](/Users/deepikapalagani/.claude/projects/-Users-deepikapalagani-Documents-nl2sql-databao/memory/nl2sql-judge-selection-findings.md)), and the published selectors were measured against majority voting, not against a strong reasoning judge. Fine-tuning also moves pair accuracy far more than end-to-end accuracy. R³-SQL's +11.2 pair-accuracy points became **+1.63 execution points**; a trained pairwise reward model used alone lost to majority voting in SIRIUS-SQL, 74.32 versus 75.03 overall and 58.62 versus 62.07 on challenging questions ([SIRIUS-SQL](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.01246)); and in our own pools JEV-27B's accuracy comes from its Qwen3.8-27B base, with its judge fine-tuning adding nothing ([judge findings](/Users/deepikapalagani/.claude/projects/-Users-deepikapalagani-Documents-nl2sql-databao/memory/nl2sql-judge-selection-findings.md)). Together these put a realistic target at **+1 to +4 questions over 82–83**, recovering 10–35% of the 11–12 misses. The top of that range assumes that on-policy minority-correct pairs fix correlated misreadings, which no SQL paper has tested, and the bottom reaches zero if the pairs are off-policy or their minority labels are noisy. The bar is also not 82, because weighting the 35B judge and JEV-27B equally already reached **85** on the latest run with no training.

The addressable ceiling sits below 11–12, because some misses rest on gold that a judge cannot legitimately learn. The Snowflake versions of 36 local questions belong to a split where 62.8% of checkable golds were wrong ([Jin et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.08778)); at least 15 local tasks have open public complaints about wrong or ambiguous gold, including fan-out inside the local029 gold and an unspecified tie-break ([#216](https://github.com/xlang-ai/Spider2/issues/216); [#160](https://github.com/xlang-ai/Spider2/issues/160)); and the team's own list flags 8 gold-sensitive tasks, among them a gold with a header and zero rows and a 12-month question over four months of data ([gold_sensitive_tasks.yaml](/Users/deepikapalagani/Documents/nl2sql-databao/nl2sql-v2/scripts/gold_sensitive_tasks.yaml)). How many of the 11–12 misses fall on these tasks should be checked before the effort is sized.

Detecting the gain is as hard as getting it. Two runs of one configuration on the 135 differ by about 3 points, roughly 4 questions, with four lanes ([CLAUDE.md](/Users/deepikapalagani/Documents/nl2sql-databao/CLAUDE.md)), and the 35B+JEV ensemble's +5–7 over 540 pools was not significant. The primary evidence should therefore come from non-Spider2 pools on databases disjoint from training, namely LiveSQLBench SQLite, Mini-Interact and the held-out trap databases, where hundreds of minority-correct pairs can be counted and three numbers tracked: order-consistent accuracy on minority-correct pairs, selection accuracy under the production round-robin rule, and the false-override rate on majority-correct pools. On the 135, the comparison should use all four run sets (540 pools), count per-question flips in each direction with a sign test, and stratify by the number of correct candidates (1, 2 or 3 of 4) and by the 43–49 questions on overlapping databases versus the rest, so that residual leakage shows up as a gap between groups. The first step is a pilot of about 100 questions per source to measure how often the agent produces minority-correct pools and how often their labels survive the second check; neither number exists yet, and together they decide whether 10–25K verified pairs are reachable.

## Conclusion

The binding constraint is not a shortage of questions but a shortage of certified minority-correct pools. The data that teaches a judge to overturn a wrong majority sits exactly where gold labels are least trustworthy and where majority-vote verification, the default in synthetic pipelines, fails by design, so the real engineering lies in the second label check, the discrimination test and the perturbed databases rather than in question generators. That reframes the synthetic route as well: verified trap items repay their cost even if fine-tuning disappoints, because they become the non-Spider2, per-error-type judge benchmark that no published selector reports and that 135 questions cannot provide at useful resolution.

The deeper issue is correlation. A judge from the agent's own family fails where the agent fails, and the only training data aimed at that shared blind spot is the agent's own outvoted-correct pools, an approach with strong indirect support from math and code but no direct SQL test. The proportionate next step is a measured pilot rather than a 25K-pair build: confirm the pool yield, train one GRPO round, and spend further only if held-out order-consistent accuracy on minority-correct pairs improves enough to beat the training-free ensemble. The same test-gated items would later serve the larger bottleneck too, since 41 of the 135 questions still have no correct candidate to select.
